

Alphabet Lost $410 Billion in One Day While Google Cloud Doubled, and the AI Capex Bill Is Why

Vizaca Editorial

Published on: Oct 30, 2026

Photo: Panumas Nik via Pexels.

Alphabet shares fell 12.6% on October 29, 2026, erasing roughly $410 billion of market value in a day, even though Google Cloud revenue more than doubled. The reason investors sold is the size of the bill: AI capital spending is now so large that Alphabet burned cash in the quarter, and Microsoft is spending at a similar pace. For startups that build on these platforms, the results show both the opportunity and the risk.

The figures below come from company results as reported by Stock Titan, Motley Fool, Vested Finance and AI Weekly. Where outlets differ, we note it. Check the filings before relying on any number for a financial decision.

Key takeaways: Google Cloud revenue rose 103% to $30.71 billion. Alphabet missed on earnings and slightly on revenue, and free cash flow was negative $9.07 billion. Capex was $51.36 billion in the quarter. Alphabet’s 2026 capex guidance is $195 billion to $205 billion. Microsoft spent $39.4 billion on capex, about double a year earlier.

Earnings per share were $2.12 on a GAAP basis against consensus near $3.02, the first miss of its kind in years. Stock Titan attributes a large part of the gap to an equity stake loss of about $8.7 billion, while Motley Fool puts a similar non-operating loss related to a SpaceX stake at $8.2 billion and also notes a $5.2 billion cash payment for an EU antitrust fine. The numbers vary by outlet, but the pattern is the same: accounting items and heavy spending hid a very strong cloud quarter.

The cloud story is the headline that investors set aside. Revenue doubled, margin rose by more than 12 points and backlog, the contracted future revenue, was reported at $514 billion at the end of June, with a Motley Fool analysis putting it at $561 billion this quarter. CEO Sundar Pichai said Google’s first party model APIs now process nearly 32 billion tokens per minute, about four times a year ago, and the Gemini app has reached 1 billion users.

Why Investors Sold Anyway

GOOGL closed at $283.82, down 12.6% from $324.89, which Stock Titan counts as the largest one day drop since its 2004 listing, beating an 11.6% decline on March 16, 2020. Three concerns drove it.

Cash. Operating cash flow was $42.29 billion, but capex of $51.36 billion pushed free cash flow negative for a second quarter in a row. Trailing twelve month free cash flow fell to $19.75 billion from $73.55 billion. Alphabet sold $28.7 billion of notes and raised $12.4 billion from share issuance in the quarter, and its long term debt reached $125.1 billion.

Search. Search growth slowed to 12%, and analysts at UBS said the results would revive fears that AI tools eat into search advertising. D.A. Davidson’s Gil Luria said investors will worry AI “has reached Google,” while cautioning against drawing conclusions from one quarter.

Depreciation to come. Spending on data centers lands on the income statement gradually as depreciation, so today’s capex becomes tomorrow’s margin pressure. Motley Fool argued the market is pricing in that future cost now.

Microsoft’s contracted backlog of $677 billion shows how much future revenue customers have committed to. A filing also shows about $10 billion in quarterly revenue from arrangements with OpenAI, which AI Weekly puts at nearly 11% of total revenue, a sign of both demand and customer concentration. Reports differ on Microsoft’s calendar 2026 capex plan, citing roughly $170 billion to $175 billion.

Putting the Numbers in Perspective

Alphabet’s guidance of $195 billion to $205 billion and Microsoft’s roughly $170 billion to $175 billion add up to about $365 billion to $380 billion for 2026, close to $1 billion a day from two companies. Alphabet’s nine month capex of $132.0 billion implies roughly $63 billion to $73 billion in the fourth quarter to hit its range. Alphabet also said capex will increase significantly in 2027, and one summary reported a figure above $300 billion, which we could not confirm in the company’s release.

For comparison, our report on third quarter startup funding shows that even the largest venture rounds are small next to a single quarter of hyperscaler spending. That scale gap is why startups increasingly rent capacity instead of building their own, and why Modal’s $750 million Series D and similar rounds focus on AI infrastructure.

The Wider Supply Chain

The spending is lifting suppliers far beyond chip designers. Sandisk reported revenue of $11.03 billion, up 378%, with $4.8 billion from data centers on AI inference demand. Reports also say Nvidia may guarantee debt for a 460 megawatt Anthropic data center buildout by Nscale, and the Department of Energy announced a $5 billion loan to Constellation for nuclear capacity contracted to Google, Amazon and Meta. Memory, power and financing now limit AI growth as much as models do. Our coverage of Meta’s Q3 results shows the same pressure at another giant.

Is This a Bubble?

The best argument against a bubble is demand: Google Cloud doubled, Azure grew 48%, Microsoft’s backlog is $677 billion and token volume grew about fourfold. The best argument for caution is that cash flow is turning negative, debt is rising and investors will punish disappointment. Twilio beat revenue expectations at $1.56 billion but fell more than 15% on a cautious fourth quarter forecast, according to AI Weekly. The market rewards visible growth and punishes doubt, and it is doing both at once.

What This Means for Startups

Capacity should expand and pricing should get more competitive. Hundreds of billions in new infrastructure usually push providers to compete for developers with credits and lower prices. That is an opinion about direction, not a guarantee, so negotiate and compare providers.

Platform dependence is the larger risk. If your product runs on one provider’s models, you inherit its pricing, rate limits and strategy. Design for model swaps and test a second provider before you need one.

Unit economics decide who survives. Token volume that grows fourfold in a year means customers will expect more AI at the same price. Track cost per user and cost per task monthly, and tie pricing to value delivered, not raw usage.

Rates and spending are linked. Heavy AI investment is part of why the Fed is watching inflation, as we explain in our report on the Fed’s October rate decision. If borrowing costs stay high, funding for smaller startups may tighten even as the largest companies keep spending.

Find the gaps the giants leave. With platforms racing to build general capability, startups can win in narrow, regulated or workflow heavy markets where trust and domain knowledge matter more than scale.

A Six Step Cost Plan for AI Startups

First, measure the cost of each AI request in your product. Second, set a target gross margin and check it monthly. Third, route simple tasks to cheaper models and reserve premium models for hard ones. Fourth, negotiate committed spend or credits instead of paying list prices. Fifth, keep an alternative provider tested and ready. Sixth, price on outcomes, so a drop in model costs improves your margin instead of forcing a price cut.

What to Watch Next

Three signals will show whether the spending pays off. The first is the Q3 filing for Alphabet, which will report the September 30 backlog figure. The second is cloud growth: if Azure and Google Cloud keep growing at these rates, the investment looks justified. The third is power and chips, where deals like the Constellation loan show what can actually be built and how fast.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why did Alphabet stock fall after Q3 earnings?

Shares fell 12.6% because earnings and revenue missed estimates, free cash flow turned negative at minus $9.07 billion, Search growth slowed to 12% and investors worried about the cost of AI spending, despite 103% growth in Google Cloud.

How much will Alphabet spend on capex in 2026?

Guidance is $195 billion to $205 billion. Alphabet also said capex will increase significantly in 2027.

How fast is Azure growing?

Azure grew 48% in Microsoft’s fiscal first quarter, and Microsoft’s commercial backlog was $677 billion, up 73%.

Is the AI spending boom good for startups?

It can lower the barrier to building AI products, but it increases dependence on a few platforms. Founders should plan for both.

What is capex?

Capital expenditure, money spent on long lived assets such as data centers, servers and chips, as opposed to day to day operating costs.
